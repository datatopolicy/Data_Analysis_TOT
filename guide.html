How to Use This Guide

This guide is the single reference for anyone facilitating the Data Analysis and Report Writing Training of Trainers (TOT). It is written for a lead facilitator and one co-facilitator working as a pair. Read it cover to cover at least two weeks before the course, then use the day tabs and session plans as your run-of-show during delivery.

Conventions used in this guide

  • Session plans: every session on the authoritative agenda has a full plan — time block, session title (verbatim from the agenda), 3–4 learning objectives, key content with the actual substance you will teach, a minute-by-minute activity plan, materials, facilitator notes, and a TRAINER TIP box on how participants will teach this back home.
  • Facilitator notes: common pitfalls, pacing tips, and what to do when participants finish early or run late. Read the notes for the next session during every tea and lunch break.
  • TRAINER TIP boxes: explain how the 24 participants — who will become in-country trainers — can adapt and re-teach each topic in Bangladesh, Côte d'Ivoire, Rwanda, and Tanzania.
  • Demo data references: all examples use the fictional country "Demoland" and its 12 fictional districts. Patterns are described qualitatively (e.g., Westbrook's rising TB cases) — never quote exact numbers from memory; always say "as shown in the demo dataset" and read values live from the file so your numbers match the answer keys.

Course Overview

Background: the Data for Health Initiative

The Data for Health (D4H) Initiative, funded by Bloomberg Philanthropies and implemented with the CDC Foundation, partners with governments to strengthen the systems that generate, analyze, and use public-health data — with a particular emphasis on civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) and routine health information systems such as DHIS2. Better data on births, deaths, and causes of death is the foundation for better policy: governments cannot reduce what they cannot count.

Within D4H, the Data Analysis Project builds national capacity to turn raw health data into accurate, equity-focused, decision-ready reports. Experience across D4H countries shows that data collection has often outpaced data use — registries and DHIS2 instances hold years of data that are rarely cleaned, rarely disaggregated, and rarely written up. This project closes that gap by training government analysts to clean data in Excel, compute standard indicators, visualize results honestly, and write reports that policymakers actually read.

Goal of the Data Analysis Project

To institutionalize sustainable, equity-focused data analysis and report-writing capacity inside ministries of health and national statistics offices, so that every country can routinely produce high-quality health reports from its own CRVS and routine data systems.

The eight project objectives

  1. Strengthen national capacity in descriptive epidemiology using tools already available in government offices, principally Microsoft Excel.
  2. Improve the completeness, quality, and documented use of CRVS data, including cause-of-death information.
  3. Increase the routine analysis and use of DHIS2 and other routine health information system data for program monitoring.
  4. Embed health-equity analysis — disaggregation by sex, age, geography, and other PROGRESS-Plus dimensions — as standard practice in every report.
  5. Institutionalize standard operating procedures (SOPs), data dictionaries, and report templates for data access, cleaning, analysis, and writing.
  6. Build a cadre of certified national trainers able to cascade data-analysis training to subnational staff.
  7. Improve translation of analytic findings into clear, actionable recommendations for policymakers and program managers.
  8. Establish monitoring and evaluation of data-use practices so progress can be tracked and course-corrected.

The seven project phases

The Data Analysis Project runs in seven phases. This TOT course is Phase 1 — everything else builds on the trainers it produces.

Phase Name What happens
1 Training of Trainers (this course) 24 mid-level government professionals (6 each from Bangladesh, Côte d'Ivoire, Rwanda, and Tanzania) master descriptive analysis in Excel, equity-focused reporting, and adult training methods.
2 Country adaptation & cascade training National teams adapt the curriculum, translate materials where needed, and train subnational analysts in-country.
3 Supervised country data-analysis projects Country teams analyze their own national datasets with remote mentoring from the project technical team.

They are mid-level government health professionals — typically holding a master's degree with 3+ years of experience — who already work with health data. After this TOT they will become the in-country trainers for Phase 2 cascade trainings, so every session should be delivered with one eye on content mastery and the other on how they will re-teach it.

Course Learning Objectives

By the end of the five days, participants will be able to:

  1. Explain the Data for Health Initiative, the Data Analysis Project's goal, objectives and phases, and their own role as national trainers in Phase 2 cascade trainings.
  2. Apply a health-equity lens to data analysis using the PROGRESS-Plus framework, identifying which dimensions of inequality their country's data can and cannot illuminate.
  3. Set precise, answerable data-analysis objectives that link a policy or program question to the right data, indicators, and disaggregations.
  4. Clean real-world-style messy datasets in Excel (Text-to-Columns, Remove Duplicates, TRIM, find-and-replace, validation) and combine datasets with VLOOKUP/XLOOKUP so analysis starts from trustworthy data.
  5. Calculate core descriptive indicators in Excel — counts, proportions, incidence and notification rates using census denominators, means, medians, and measures of spread — and choose the right summary for the data at hand.
  6. Use pivot tables to produce stratified analyses (by sex, age group, district, year) efficiently and reproducibly.
  7. Structure a technical report using IMRAD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion), and draft policy-ready recommendations.
  8. Design honest, publication-quality tables, figures, and district maps in Excel following best-practice rules for clarity and accessibility.
  9. Produce and present a 10-slide country-team mini analysis report from synthetic data, and defend its methods and conclusions under questioning.
  10. Plan and deliver a cascade training session back home using adult-learning training methodologies (demonstration, guided practice, feedback).

Monitoring & Evaluation Framework

Pre/post-test plan

A written knowledge test is administered twice: Monday 9:00 during welcome (pre-test, 30 minutes) and Friday 16:00 during wrap-up (post-test, 30 minutes). The two forms are parallel — same blueprint, different item wording — so score gains reflect learning, not memorization.

  • Format: 25 multiple-choice questions (1 point each) + 2 short-answer questions (5 points each) = 35 points total.
  • Blueprint: 5 items on D4H/project concepts and equity; 5 on data cleaning and combining datasets; 5 on rates, descriptive statistics, and pivot tables; 5 on IMRAD, tables, figures, maps, and recommendations; 5 on training methodologies. Short answers: (1) compute and interpret an incidence rate from a small table; (2) critique a flawed chart and rewrite its takeaway.
  • Pass standard: post-test score of 70% (24.5/35) plus demonstrated competence in the group project. The test measures knowledge; the group project measures applied skill.
  • Logistics: print 30 copies of each form; participants write a 4-digit anonymous code (last 4 digits of phone number) on both forms so pre/post can be matched without names. Co-facilitator scores within one week; aggregate results feed the course report.
  • Use of results: report mean gain and % reaching the pass standard overall and by country team. Items missed by >50% of participants flag topics needing reinforcement in Phase 2 cascade trainings.

Daily evaluation forms

A one-page form is distributed at 16:45 each day and collected before participants leave. It asks: (1) the most useful thing learned today (free text); (2) one thing still unclear (free text); (3) pace rating (too slow / about right / too fast); (4) confidence to teach today's hardest topic back home (1–5 scale). The co-facilitator tallies responses each evening; the lead facilitator addresses the top 2–3 unclear items in the next morning's debrief. This is the course's early-warning system — use it.

Group Project Guidance

The assignment

Working in their 4 country teams of 6, participants produce a 10-slide mini analysis report using the Demoland synthetic datasets, presented on Friday morning (Day 5, 9:30–11:30). Each team chooses one analytic focus from the menu below — first-come, first-served at the end of Day 1, so all four topics are covered across teams:

  1. TB trends and equity, 2021–2025: national trend, Westbrook's rise, sex and age patterns, district comparison.
  2. TB case notification rates using census denominators: which districts carry the highest burden once population size is accounted for?
  3. Mortality registration completeness: quantify and visualize Riverside's under-registration and discuss implications for interpreting mortality indicators.
  4. Subnational disparities in TB burden: rank districts, map the burden, and identify priority districts for intensified case finding. Each 10-slide report must contain: title slide; analysis objectives (1 slide); methods and data sources including cleaning steps (1–2 slides); results with at least 2 publication-quality tables and 2 figures (4–5 slides); equity interpretation (1 slide); recommendations (1 slide). Presentations are 15 minutes + 5 minutes of questions.

Day 1 (Mon 22 Mar) | 11:00–12:00

Principles of Equity

Learning objectives

  1. Define health equity and distinguish it from equality.
  2. List the PROGRESS-Plus dimensions and apply them to a health dataset.
  3. Explain why disaggregation is the core analytic habit of equity.
  4. Identify what a dataset cannot reveal about equity and state it honestly.

Key content and talking points

Open with the definition. Health equity means everyone has a fair and just opportunity to be as healthy as possible — which requires removing obstacles such as poverty, discrimination, and unequal access to care. Equity is not equality: equality gives everyone the same thing; equity gives people what they need to reach the same outcome. Draw the classic three-panels graphic (boxes/fence) on the flipchart — it lands in every culture.

Introduce PROGRESS-Plus as the standard checklist for asking 'who might be left behind?' in any dataset:

Dimension What it captures Example in health data
P — Place of residence Urban/rural, district, distance to facility Westbrook vs. national TB trend in Demoland
R — Race / ethnicity Ethnic or racial group Often unrecorded — note the gap honestly
O — Occupation Job type, informal labor, health workers Miners and TB risk
G — Gender / sex Sex and gender ~60–65% male TB burden in Demoland
R — Religion Religious affiliation Affects care-seeking in some settings
E — Education Literacy, schooling Treatment adherence gradients
S — Socioeconomic status Wealth, income, insurance Catastrophic TB costs
S — Social capital Networks, support, marginalization Migrants' access to DOT
Plus: age Age groups TB concentration in working ages
Plus: disability Disability status Barriers to facility access

Three analytic habits to drill all week:

  1. Disaggregate by default — never present only a national average. In Demoland, the national TB decline hides Westbrook's rise; the average is the lie that equity analysis corrects.
  2. Name what the data cannot show — if ethnicity or wealth is not recorded, say so in Methods and Limitations rather than pretending the analysis is complete.
  3. Interpret gaps as signals, not noise — Riverside's incomplete mortality registration is a finding about the system, and the recommendation writes itself (strengthen registration there). Close with the week's equity rule: every table, every figure, every report this week is stratified by at least sex and district, and every discussion section names who might be missing from the data.

Group Projects: Country teams report on SOPs and challenges accessing data

Learning objectives

  1. Define SOP and its role in reliable data access.
  2. Report their country's SOP landscape and top data-access barrier.
  3. Compare barriers and enablers across the four countries.

Key content and talking points

Frame: the best analysis in the world cannot happen if analysts cannot get the data. A Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) is a written, step-by-step instruction for a routine task — here: requesting, accessing, cleaning, storing, and sharing health data. Good SOPs name the data owner, the request route, the approval timeline, the format delivered, and the confidentiality rules.

Each country team reports (5 minutes each): Do you have written SOPs for data access? Who owns CRVS/DHIS2 data in your setting? What is the single biggest barrier — legal, bureaucratic, technical, or trust? One scribe per team captures barriers on flipchart.

Facilitator synthesizes across the four countries: common barriers (unclear ownership, paper-to-digital gaps, no data dictionaries, fear of blame when data look bad) and common enablers (a named data focal point, MOUs between agencies, scheduled data-release calendars). Link forward: project objective 5 is institutionalizing SOPs, and Thursday's report-template session returns to documentation.

Cleaning data in Excel

Learning objectives

  1. Execute the standard 8-step cleaning sequence on a messy dataset.
  2. Use Text-to-Columns, TRIM, Remove Duplicates, and Find & Replace correctly.
  3. Apply Data Validation to prevent future entry errors.
  4. Maintain a cleaning log suitable for a Methods section.

Key content and talking points

Frame with the rule: every hour of cleaning saves three hours of arguing about wrong results. Dirty data is not a moral failing — the Demoland messy file is typical of real DHIS2/CRVS extracts. Teach the standard cleaning sequence, demonstrating each step live on DA-TOT_Exercise_Messy-DHIS2-Extract.xlsx before participants replicate:

  1. Inspect first: sort each column, scan for blanks, odd values, and inconsistent spellings (e.g., 'Westbrook' vs 'westbrook ' vs 'West brook'). Never clean what you have not looked at.
  2. TRIM leading/trailing spaces: =TRIM(A2), then Paste Special → Values over the original. Spaces are the commonest cause of failed lookups.
  3. Text-to-Columns (Data tab): split combined fields (e.g., 'Westbrook_2024' or 'M;25-34') using delimiters; also converts text-numbers to numbers (choose General format on the last wizard step).
  4. Remove Duplicates (Data tab): select the full table first, then tick only the columns that define a true duplicate (e.g., district + year + sex + age group) — removing on all columns can delete valid rows.
  5. Standardize categories with Find & Replace (Ctrl+H): unify district spellings and sex codes ('M'/'Male'/'m'). Replace in a copy, verify counts before/after with COUNTIF.
  6. Fix dates: ensure Excel recognizes them as dates (they right-align); use Text-to-Columns with Date format or =DATEVALUE for text dates; flag impossible dates (e.g., 32/13/2024) for the source team.
  7. Validate: Data → Data Validation to lock categorical columns to a list (district names, sex), preventing future typos at entry.
  8. Document everything in a cleaning log: file name, date, each step, rows before/after. The log goes into the report's Methods section. Key cautions: always work on a copy (never the raw extract); keep the raw file untouched so cleaning is reproducible; check row counts before and after each destructive step; and treat Riverside-style incompleteness as a data-quality finding to report, not missing cells to silently fill.

Combining data sets in Excel

Learning objectives

  1. Merge a numerator file with a denominator file using a shared key.
  2. Write correct VLOOKUP (exact match), XLOOKUP, and INDEX/MATCH formulas.
  3. Diagnose and fix failed matches (spaces, spelling, missing keys).

Key content and talking points

Analysis needs numerators and denominators in one table: TB cases from the notification file, populations from the census file. Teach lookup functions as 'asking one table a question using a shared key' (here: district, or district + sex).

VLOOKUP — the classic. Syntax: =VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index_num, [range_lookup]). Demo: pull district population next to TB cases. Non-negotiable rules: the lookup column must be the leftmost column of table_array; always use FALSE (exact match) for merging datasets — TRUE sorts-and-approximates and silently returns wrong neighbors; wrap in IFERROR for missing keys: =IFERROR(VLOOKUP(...),"check").

XLOOKUP — the modern replacement (Excel 2021+/Microsoft 365). Syntax: =XLOOKUP(lookup_value, lookup_array, return_array, [if_not_found]). Advantages: looks left or right, defaults to exact match, built-in not-found handling. Demo the same merge in one cleaner formula.

INDEX/MATCH — the fallback for Excel 2016/2019 without XLOOKUP: =INDEX(return_array, MATCH(lookup_value, lookup_array, 0)). More flexible than VLOOKUP and immune to inserted columns.

Join hygiene: confirm keys match exactly (recall the TRIM lesson — 'Westbrook ' will not match 'Westbrook'); check for unmatched keys with COUNTIF/IFERROR flags; verify totals before and after the merge; never merge on names alone when codes exist.

Accessing and using census data to calculate rates in Excel

Learning objectives

  1. Explain why counts must be converted to rates for fair comparison.
  2. Distinguish incidence rate, case notification rate, and proportion.
  3. Compute a case notification rate in Excel from the merged Demoland files.
  4. State the rules for matching numerators to denominators.

Key content and talking points

The central epidemiologic idea of the morning: a count without a denominator is just a number; a rate is information. Comparing raw TB case counts across Demoland's districts misleads — a big district will always 'lead'. Rates divide by the population at risk so districts can be compared fairly.

Teach the three related measures precisely:

Measure Formula When to use it
Incidence rate (new cases ÷ population at risk) × multiplier (e.g., 100,000) New TB cases arising in a defined population over a period — the true risk measure.
Case notification rate (notified cases ÷ population) × 100,000 What surveillance actually captures; the workhorse indicator when, as in Demoland, we count notifications.
Proportion / percentage (part ÷ whole) × 100 Share of a whole — e.g., % of TB cases that are male (~60–65% in Demoland). Not a rate: no population at risk, no time.

Worked mini-example (do this live on the flipchart, then in Excel): Suppose a district notifies 240 new TB cases in 2024 and its census population is 300,000. Case notification rate = (240 ÷ 300,000) × 100,000 = 80 per 100,000 population. Then ask: a second district notifies 200 cases in a population of 100,000 → (200 ÷ 100,000) × 100,000 = 200 per 100,000. Fewer cases, far higher burden — this is why rates exist. Have participants replicate with Demoland districts from the merged file, reading values live from the dataset.

  • Choose the multiplier to avoid tiny decimals: per 100,000 for TB, per 1,000 for common vital events. Always state it ('per 100,000 population').
  • Match numerator to denominator in person, place, and time: 2024 cases over the 2024 census population, same district, same sex/age strata when stratifying.
  • Never compute mortality rates from Riverside's data without flagging incompleteness — a rate built on under-registered deaths understates mortality. Teach the integrity check first, the rate second.
  • Excel mechanics: rate column = (cases_cell / pop_cell)* 100000; lock the multiplier in a named cell; format to 1 decimal; label the column 'TB notification rate per 100,000 (2024)'.

Descriptive statistics review

Learning objectives

  1. Define mean, median, mode, range, IQR, and standard deviation.
  2. Select the appropriate summary for symmetric vs. skewed vs. categorical data.
  3. Explain why the mean alone can mislead when outliers are present.
  4. State the limits of descriptive statistics.

Key content and talking points

Conceptual session — no laptops open. Descriptive statistics summarize data; they do not explain causes. Teach the two families:

Measures of central tendency — 'what is typical?'

Measure Definition When to use it Caution
Mean Sum of values ÷ number of values Symmetric, continuous data (e.g., average age of TB cases when roughly normal) Pulled by outliers — one very old case shifts it
Median Middle value when sorted Skewed data or data with outliers (e.g., district rates when one district spikes) Hides the shape of the distribution
Mode Most frequent value Categorical data (most common age group, most affected district) A dataset can have two modes or none

Measures of spread — 'how much does it vary?'

Measure Definition When to use it
Range Maximum − minimum Quick sense of span; very sensitive to extremes
Interquartile range (IQR) 75th percentile − 25th percentile Spread of the middle half; robust partner to the median
Standard deviation (SD) Typical distance of values from the mean With the mean for symmetric data; ~68% of values fall within ±1 SD when roughly normal

The decision rule to drill: plot or at least sort the data first; if symmetric → mean + SD; if skewed or with outliers → median + IQR; if categorical → mode and proportions. Choosing mean + SD for everything is the commonest beginner error — demonstrate with Demoland district rates: Westbrook's rise skews the picture, so the median district rate tells a truer 'typical district' story than the mean.

Close with intellectual honesty: descriptive statistics describe; they do not test hypotheses or prove cause. That boundary is what keeps reports credible.

IMRAD Structure

Learning objectives

  1. State the purpose of each IMRAD section.
  2. Sort content correctly across the four sections (border policy).
  3. Explain why Methods must be reproducible.
  4. Draft an IMRAD outline for the team project.

Key content and talking points

IMRAD — Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion — is the international standard skeleton of a technical report. Teach it as four rooms, each with one job and a strict border policy:

Section Purpose — answers... Contains Never contains
Introduction Why does this analysis matter? Background, policy context, and the analysis objectives. Ends with the objectives verbatim. Results or methods detail.
Methods What did you do, exactly? Data sources (Demoland files, years), cleaning steps (the cleaning log!), indicator definitions and formulas, denominators, stratifications, software (Excel version), limitations of the data. Any finding — no numbers that belong in Results.
Results What did you find? Text + tables + figures, ordered by the objectives. Every table/figure is introduced in text and has a takeaway sentence. No interpretation beyond describing. Interpretation, recommendations, or new data.
(and) Discussion What does it mean, and what should we do? Interpretation of key findings, comparison with expectations, equity implications (who is left behind?), limitations, and recommendations. New results that were never shown in Results.
  • The border policy is the lesson: results in Methods confuse; new data in Discussion destroys credibility. Reviewers check the borders first.
  • Methods must be reproducible: a competent analyst reading only the Methods should be able to rebuild the analysis — that is why Monday's cleaning log and the exact rate formulas live here.
  • The Discussion earns its keep with two moves: 'so what?' (interpretation for policy) and 'what now?' (recommendations). Thursday's sessions deepen both.
  • Abstract/executive summary comes last in writing, first in reading — one paragraph per IMRAD section, written after the body is final. Best practices for creating tables

Learning objectives

  1. State the 10 table best-practice rules.
  2. Write a self-contained table title with units.
  3. Rebuild a default Excel output into a publication-quality table.

Key content and talking points

Tables carry precise values; figures carry patterns. A table's job is to let a reader find any number in seconds and trust all of them. Teach the rules as a checklist — participants will apply it within the hour:

  1. Title above the table, self-contained: 'Table 1. TB case notification rate per 100,000 population by district, Demoland, 2024.' A reader should understand it without the text.
  2. One idea per table. If it needs two titles, it needs two tables.
  3. Columns: variables across the top, categories down the rows; order rows meaningfully (highest to lowest rate, or chronological) — never alphabetical by default.
  4. Units in the title or column header ('per 100,000'), never buried in a footnote the reader must hunt for.
  5. Align numbers right (or on the decimal point); align text left. Same decimals throughout a column (e.g., 80.4, 112.0 — never 80.43 next to 112).
  6. No vertical gridlines; minimal horizontal lines (top, under header, bottom). In Excel: strip the default gridlines, add borders only where the rule requires.
  7. Totals where they aid interpretation — and label whether a total row is a sum or a weighted rate (a 'total' of rates is meaningless; show the national rate computed from national totals).
  8. Footnotes for: data source, definitions, missing data (e.g., 'Riverside mortality data incomplete — see Methods'), and any suppression of small numbers.
  9. Round sensibly: rates to 1 decimal, percentages to whole numbers in text. False precision (4 decimals) signals innumeracy, not rigor.
  10. In Excel: build the table as values (Paste Special → Values) once calculations are final, so sorting and formatting cannot break formulas; keep the formula version on a separate 'working' sheet. Demonstrate live: take an ugly default Excel dump of district rates and rebuild it rule by rule on the projector — participants audibly react when the same numbers become twice as readable. Before/after is the pedagogy.

Best practices for creating figures

Learning objectives

  1. Match chart type to the message using the message→chart table.
  2. Write takeaway titles and honest axes (zero baseline for bars).
  3. Apply decluttering and color-blind-safe design in Excel.
  4. State the accessibility requirements for figures.

Key content and talking points

Figures show patterns; tables show values. Choose the chart from the message, not from habit:

Message Chart type Rule
Trend over time Line chart One line per group max 3–4; label lines directly (no legend hunt); y-axis labeled with units
Compare categories Bar chart (or column) Y-axis starts at ZERO — truncated bars lie about differences; order bars meaningfully
Part of a whole Bar chart (avoid pie) Pies fail beyond 2–3 slices; a sorted bar shows the ~60–65% male share more honestly
Distribution/shape Histogram Bin sensibly; label axes; never a 3D effect
Two variables' relationship Scatter plot No connecting lines unless time-ordered
  1. Title as takeaway: 'Figure 1. TB notification rates declined nationally but rose in Westbrook, Demoland, 2021–2025.' The title states the finding; the subtitle or footnote carries the method detail.
  2. Zero baseline for bars; labeled, unbroken axes for lines. Truncation is the commonest visual lie — demonstrate with Westbrook vs. national bars at y=0 vs. y=50 and let the room feel the deception.
  3. Direct labeling beats legends; data labels only where they add information (endpoints of lines, not every bar).
  4. Color with purpose: one accent color for the finding (Westbrook's line), grey for context; color-blind-safe palettes (blue/orange, not red/green); every figure must survive black-and-white printing.
  5. Declutter: remove gridlines or fade them, kill 3D effects, no chart junk. In Excel: Chart Design → Add Chart Element sparingly; set gridlines to light grey or none.
  6. Source footnote on every figure: 'Source: Demoland synthetic TB notifications, 2021–2025.'
  7. Excel mechanics: select the data → Insert → chart type; right-click axis → Format Axis to set bounds (force 0 for bars); Chart Title → type the takeaway; use Paste Special → Picture into slides so the figure cannot shift. Accessibility is non-negotiable: sufficient color contrast, never color as the only encoding (add labels or patterns), minimum 10pt axis text. A figure a policymaker cannot read is a finding that does not exist.

Drafting appropriate recommendations

Learning objectives

  1. Apply the four tests (linked, specific, feasible, honest) to draft recommendations.
  2. Tailor recommendations to the decision-maker's mandate.
  3. Prioritize recommendations by impact and feasibility.

Key content and talking points

Recommendations are the Discussion section's payoff — and the most abused part of technical reports. Teach the four tests every recommendation must pass:

Test Question to ask Fail example → Pass example
Linked Does it follow directly from a finding in Results? Fail: 'Improve TB services.' (linked to nothing) → Pass: 'Intensify case finding in Westbrook, where notifications rose against the national decline (Fig. 1).'
Specific Who does what, where, by when? Fail: 'Strengthen registration.' → Pass: 'Deploy two mobile registration teams to Riverside in Q3 2027; target: reduce unregistered deaths, re-measured in Q1 2028.'
Feasible Can the audience actually do it with available resources and authority? Fail: recommending a new national survey to a district manager → Pass: actions within the reader's mandate.
Honest Does it stay inside what the data can support? Fail: 'Screening caused the decline' (no causal data) → Pass: 'The decline coincided with X; evaluate X's contribution before scaling.'
  • Write for the reader's desk: a minister needs 3 prioritized actions with costs; a program manager needs operational detail. One report can carry both — executive summary for the former, Discussion for the latter.
  • Prioritize ruthlessly: no more than 3–5 recommendations; rank by impact × feasibility. Twelve recommendations = zero recommendations.
  • Name the evidence gap: 'We cannot recommend Y until Z is measured' is itself a valuable recommendation — it commissions the next analysis.
  • Equity test: each recommendation should state who benefits — '...reducing the male–female gap in case detection' — or it risks widening inequity by helping the already-reached. Descriptive statistics Numbers summarizing data (mean, median, SD, counts, percentages); they describe but do not test hypotheses or prove cause.
DHIS2 District Health Information Software 2 — open-source routine health information system used in 70+ countries for aggregate facility data.

Morbidity Burden of disease/illness in a population (as distinct from mortality).

Numerator The count of events (cases, deaths) placed over the denominator in an indicator.

Pivot table Excel engine for rapid stratified summaries: drag fields to rows/columns/values; with slicers it re-stratifies in one click.
Prevalence Proportion of a population with a condition at a given time; distinct from incidence (new cases over a period).

Range Maximum minus minimum; quickest but crudest measure of spread.

Remove Duplicates (Excel) Data-tab tool deleting repeated rows; select the full table first and tick only the columns defining a true duplicate.

Slicer (Excel) Clickable filter buttons for a pivot table; one click re-stratifies the analysis.

Standard deviation (SD) Typical distance of values from the mean; reported with the mean for symmetric data.
Stratification See disaggregation: analysis within subgroups to reveal differences hidden by averages.
