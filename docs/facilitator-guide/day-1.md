## Day 1 (Mon 22 Mar)  |  11:00–12:00

### Principles of Equity

### Learning objectives

1. Define health equity and distinguish it from equality.
1. List the PROGRESS-Plus dimensions and apply them to a health dataset.
1. Explain why disaggregation is the core analytic habit of equity.
1. Identify what a dataset cannot reveal about equity and state it honestly.
### Key content and talking points

Open with the definition. Health equity means everyone has a fair and just opportunity to be as healthy as possible — which requires removing obstacles such as poverty, discrimination, and unequal access to care. Equity is not equality: equality gives everyone the same thing; equity gives people what they need to reach the same outcome. Draw the classic three-panels graphic (boxes/fence) on the flipchart — it lands in every culture.

Introduce PROGRESS-Plus as the standard checklist for asking 'who might be left behind?' in any dataset:

|**Dimension**|**What it captures**|**Example in health data**|
| --- | --- | --- |
| P — Place of residence | Urban/rural, district, distance to facility | Westbrook vs. national TB trend in Demoland |
| R — Race / ethnicity | Ethnic or racial group | Often unrecorded — note the gap honestly |
| O — Occupation | Job type, informal labor, health workers | Miners and TB risk |
| G — Gender / sex | Sex and gender | ~60–65% male TB burden in Demoland |
| R — Religion | Religious affiliation | Affects care-seeking in some settings |
| E — Education | Literacy, schooling | Treatment adherence gradients |
| S — Socioeconomic status | Wealth, income, insurance | Catastrophic TB costs |
| S — Social capital | Networks, support, marginalization | Migrants' access to DOT |
| Plus: age | Age groups | TB concentration in working ages |
| Plus: disability | Disability status | Barriers to facility access |

Three analytic habits to drill all week:

1. Disaggregate by default — never present only a national average. In Demoland, the national TB decline hides Westbrook's rise; the average is the lie that equity analysis corrects.
1. Name what the data cannot show — if ethnicity or wealth is not recorded, say so in Methods and Limitations rather than pretending the analysis is complete.
1. Interpret gaps as signals, not noise — Riverside's incomplete mortality registration is a finding about the system, and the recommendation writes itself (strengthen registration there).
Close with the week's equity rule: every table, every figure, every report this week is stratified by at least sex and district, and every discussion section names who might be missing from the data.

### Key content and talking points

### Establish data analysis objectives

### Learning objectives

1. Distinguish weak from strong analysis objectives using the five-part anatomy.
1. Rewrite a vague objective into a precise, answerable one.
1. Draft 2–3 analysis objectives for their team project topic.
### Key content and talking points

A data-analysis objective is a precise, answerable question that links a policy or program concern to data, indicators, and disaggregations. Vague objectives produce vague analyses. Teach the anatomy of a strong objective — Population, Indicator, Disaggregation, Timeframe, Comparison:

|**Weak objective**|**Strong objective**|**Why it is stronger**|
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analyze TB data. | Describe TB case notification trends by district, sex, and age group in Demoland, 2021–2025, and identify districts deviating from the national trend. | Names indicator, disaggregations, timeframe, and the analytic task (identify deviating districts). |
| Look at mortality. | Assess completeness of death registration by district in Demoland, 2021–2025, by comparing registered deaths against expected levels, to prioritize registration strengthening. | Defines the measure (completeness), method (vs. expected), and use (prioritization). |
| Study equity. | Compare TB notification rates between males and females and across districts in Demoland in 2024 using census denominators, to quantify sex and geographic inequities. | Specifies rate (needs denominator), comparison groups, year. |

Rules to drill: one objective = one question; name the indicator and its numerator/denominator; name every disaggregation up front (it dictates how you clean and structure the data); state the timeframe; say what decision the answer will inform. If an objective cannot be answered with the available data, rewrite the objective — do not torture the data.

Demonstrate live: take a participant-suggested vague objective and rewrite it on the flipchart using the five-part anatomy. Then teams draft 2–3 objectives for their chosen project topic — these become slide 2 of Friday's deck.

## Day 1 (Mon 22 Mar)  |  15:00–16:00

### Cleaning data in Excel

### Learning objectives

1. Execute the standard 8-step cleaning sequence on a messy dataset.
1. Use Text-to-Columns, TRIM, Remove Duplicates, and Find & Replace correctly.
1. Apply Data Validation to prevent future entry errors.
1. Maintain a cleaning log suitable for a Methods section.
### Key content and talking points

Frame with the rule: every hour of cleaning saves three hours of arguing about wrong results. Dirty data is not a moral failing — the Demoland messy file is typical of real DHIS2/CRVS extracts. Teach the standard cleaning sequence, demonstrating each step live on DA-TOT_Exercise_Messy-DHIS2-Extract.xlsx before participants replicate:

1. Inspect first: sort each column, scan for blanks, odd values, and inconsistent spellings (e.g., 'Westbrook' vs 'westbrook ' vs 'West brook'). Never clean what you have not looked at.
1. TRIM leading/trailing spaces: =TRIM(A2), then Paste Special → Values over the original. Spaces are the commonest cause of failed lookups.
1. Text-to-Columns (Data tab): split combined fields (e.g., 'Westbrook_2024' or 'M;25-34') using delimiters; also converts text-numbers to numbers (choose General format on the last wizard step).
1. Remove Duplicates (Data tab): select the full table first, then tick only the columns that define a true duplicate (e.g., district + year + sex + age group) — removing on all columns can delete valid rows.
1. Standardize categories with Find & Replace (Ctrl+H): unify district spellings and sex codes ('M'/'Male'/'m'). Replace in a copy, verify counts before/after with COUNTIF.
1. Fix dates: ensure Excel recognizes them as dates (they right-align); use Text-to-Columns with Date format or =DATEVALUE for text dates; flag impossible dates (e.g., 32/13/2024) for the source team.
1. Validate: Data → Data Validation to lock categorical columns to a list (district names, sex), preventing future typos at entry.
1. Document everything in a cleaning log: file name, date, each step, rows before/after. The log goes into the report's Methods section.
Key cautions: always work on a copy (never the raw extract); keep the raw file untouched so cleaning is reproducible; check row counts before and after each destructive step; and treat Riverside-style incompleteness as a data-quality finding to report, not missing cells to silently fill.

### Combining data sets in Excel

### Learning objectives

1. Merge a numerator file with a denominator file using a shared key.
1. Write correct VLOOKUP (exact match), XLOOKUP, and INDEX/MATCH formulas.
1. Diagnose and fix failed matches (spaces, spelling, missing keys).
### Key content and talking points

Analysis needs numerators and denominators in one table: TB cases from the notification file, populations from the census file. Teach lookup functions as 'asking one table a question using a shared key' (here: district, or district + sex).

VLOOKUP — the classic. Syntax: =VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index_num, [range_lookup]). Demo: pull district population next to TB cases. Non-negotiable rules: the lookup column must be the leftmost column of table_array; always use FALSE (exact match) for merging datasets — TRUE sorts-and-approximates and silently returns wrong neighbors; wrap in IFERROR for missing keys: =IFERROR(VLOOKUP(...),"check").

XLOOKUP — the modern replacement (Excel 2021+/Microsoft 365). Syntax: =XLOOKUP(lookup_value, lookup_array, return_array, [if_not_found]). Advantages: looks left or right, defaults to exact match, built-in not-found handling. Demo the same merge in one cleaner formula.

INDEX/MATCH — the fallback for Excel 2016/2019 without XLOOKUP: =INDEX(return_array, MATCH(lookup_value, lookup_array, 0)). More flexible than VLOOKUP and immune to inserted columns.

Join hygiene: confirm keys match exactly (recall the TRIM lesson — 'Westbrook ' will not match 'Westbrook'); check for unmatched keys with COUNTIF/IFERROR flags; verify totals before and after the merge; never merge on names alone when codes exist.
